<h2 id="how">How newsletter monetization works</h2>

Readers pay you, a sponsor pays you, or both. How to monetize a newsletter is the decision of which of those payments the software is allowed to touch.

A paid subscription is the reader payment. You set the price. The platform either takes a percent, takes nothing and bills you a plan, or cannot do the sale. How newsletters make money in the subscription case is that gap between the price and the fees, times the number of people who keep paying. Churn is not in any vendor's pricing table. Do not treat a full list as a full set of payers.

Email monetization is the wider version: a course, a booking, or a file, not only the recurring letter. Kit's help article, updated 3 September 2026 and re-read 25 November 2026, says commerce includes a paid newsletter on every plan, including Free. MailerLite's pricing file allows one digital product on Free and turns on paid newsletters only on Comfort and Power.

A sponsorship is a payment you usually invoice yourself. The letter is the ad. Any of the eight sending tools can carry that line, including MailCheap. The platform fee tables below do not apply to a sponsor check that never enters the platform. The commercial-email rules still apply to the issue. The setup page covers those rules: [how to start a newsletter](/guides/how-to-start-a-newsletter/).

An ad network is the platform finding the sponsor. beehiiv's pricing file lists the ad network on Lite and Pro, not on Free, not during the trial, and not in every region. The file does not publish a rate card. This page does not invent a CPM. If the network is why you would pay for Lite, price Lite from the [beehiiv pricing](/pricing/beehiiv/) table and treat ad revenue as unknown until beehiiv shows it in your account.

beehiiv's source is [beehiiv.com/pricing.md](https://www.beehiiv.com/pricing.md), re-read 25 November 2026. Put the monthly plan next to the percent before you call either one cheaper. Newsletter monetization at $1,000 of revenue is a $100 Substack fee and a $0 publishing plan, or a $0 cut plus Lite at $59, Publisher at $35, Comfort at $19, or Everything at $99 ($948 for the year). Ghost Starter at $18 cannot make the sale. On the $10 example, Kit's fee is about $65 and there is no monthly commerce plan on Free.

At $5,000 of revenue, Substack's 10% is $500 before Stripe. Lite at 5,000 subscribers is $109 a month. Publisher at 5,000 members is $75 a month, or $756 for the year. At $10,000 of revenue the fee is $1,000 before Stripe, against Lite at $139 or Publisher at $105 ($1,056 for the year). The percent is the larger number in those two comparisons, and the smaller one when revenue is still a few hundred dollars.

MailCheap has no percent to compute. The sending plan is the whole invoice, and the reader payment happens somewhere else. A 0% tool still has to be able to charge. MailCheap's plan cannot.

Match the fee to the payment you collect. A few one-time product sales are a Kit or MailerLite commerce question, not a Substack 10% question, until the product is a subscription. A $5 monthly price is a percent-versus-tier question, and the member count sets the tier. The [cost guide](/guides/newsletter-cost/) prices the sending plan. This page prices the cut. The [platform guide](/guides/newsletter-platforms/) says which product fits the rest of the job.

<h3 id="paid">How to start a paid newsletter on a plan that can charge</h3>

Start on a plan that lists the feature. Substack can charge on the free publishing plan, then keeps 10%. Kit can charge on Free, then keeps 3.5% plus $0.30 per USD sale. beehiiv can charge on Lite or Pro, not on Free. Ghost can charge on Publisher or Business, not on Starter. MailerLite can charge for a paid newsletter on Comfort or Power, not on Free. Flodesk can charge on Everything, not on Email or Free. MailCheap cannot charge.

Write the price next to the promise. The vendors do not set it. A range you saw on a vendor's marketing page is not a result from this site. Annual reader plans are the reader's prepay, not your annual software bill. Keep one free path while strangers are still deciding. How to make money with a newsletter, early on, is often a free issue plus one paid thing.

A CSV of addresses does not recreate a paid subscription. Ghost bills in your Stripe account. Kit uses a Kit-managed Stripe account, even if you already have one. Read that difference before you treat a move as an export.

<h3 id="model">The newsletter business model, without a forecast</h3>

A newsletter business model is the mix of readers, sponsors, and products, plus your time, which no pricing page lists. This site does not publish earnings. Newsletter monetization, on the pages here, stops at the fee and the plan.

Do newsletters make money? Some do. A fee at $10,000 a month of reader revenue is not $10,000 of profit. Refunds, the monthly plan, Stripe, and the hours to write sit under that number. At $200 a month of Substack revenue, 10% is $20 and the publishing plan is $0. A $59 Lite plan costs more than that $20 until revenue grows, or until you need Lite for something other than the percent.

Make money with a newsletter by counting those lines for your list, then reading the pair that matches the two tools you are actually choosing. [beehiiv vs Substack](/compare/beehiiv-vs-substack/) is the percent against the bucket. [Ghost vs Substack](/compare/ghost-vs-substack/) is the membership site against the percent. The switch pages, including [Substack alternatives](/alternatives/substack/), are for when you already use one of them.

<h3 id="outside">Sponsors, and checkouts that live somewhere else</h3>

You can monetize a newsletter the tool cannot bill. Put the sponsor in the issue, or link to a checkout you run elsewhere. MailCheap is that shape: a counted send, no reader paywall. Kit, MailerLite, and Flodesk can do the same. Their own commerce is optional. The choice is still newsletter monetization. It is just not their percent.

Price the sending plan on the [cost guide](/guides/newsletter-cost/). If the other checkout also takes 10%, you moved Substack's percent, you did not delete it. A pricing page cannot tell you whether a sponsor will buy the issue. It can tell you whether the tool takes a cut when the reader pays.
