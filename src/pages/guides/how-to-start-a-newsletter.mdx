<h2 id="what">1. What is a newsletter, and who is it for</h2>

A newsletter is a repeating email to people who asked for it. An email newsletter is that same thing when the point is the inbox, not a social feed. Newsletters differ by promise, not by the software logo. A shop can send one note a week about what arrived. A founder can send a product letter. A writer can send an interview series and later charge for it. Those are newsletter examples of a promise, not templates to copy. A personal newsletter is the same form with a narrower "who": people who want that person's notes, not a segment of a catalog.

One sentence is enough: who it is for, and what they get that they would not get by browsing. "A Tuesday note on independent bookstore news for people who buy from shops, not chains" is specific. "Thoughts on books and life" is only a topic. Put that sentence on the signup page. If you cannot name the next four issues, draft them before you open an account. How to set up a newsletter is the rest of this page. How to publish a newsletter on the web can wait until the first issue has gone to the inbox.

<h2 id="cadence">2. How often should you send a newsletter</h2>

Weekly is the schedule most people can keep next to a job. Daily is a different product. It multiplies the email count by about seven, and some plans that look cheap for a weekly letter run out of sends. Twice a week is a choice you should write down, not drift into.

Write the next six send days on a calendar. If two are already impossible, do not promise them. Yahoo's sender guidance says to honor the frequency people were told. A weekly list that becomes a daily list is a complaint risk, not a growth tactic.

The planning month is subscribers times issues per week times 4.33. At 1,000 people, one issue a week is about 4,300 emails. Seven issues a week is about 30,300. Kit, beehiiv, Ghost, and Flodesk's paid plans do not charge per issue. MailCheap and MailerLite Comfort do. The [cost guide](/guides/newsletter-cost/) is that table. This page uses one issue a week as the default.

A welcome email is a second send, once, to each new person. A confirmation click, if you use it, is another. Tests are extra. On MailCheap those messages count toward the monthly cap. The issue can still be once a week.

<h2 id="free">3. How to start a newsletter for free</h2>

You can start a newsletter for free when the plan's cap and its feature list both cover the first six months, not just the first afternoon.

**Kit Free** is $0 for up to 10,000 subscribers, with unlimited broadcasts, forms, and landing pages. The help article, updated 23 September 2026 and re-read 25 November 2026, says sequences, visual automations, rules, and RSS need a paid plan. You can sell a digital product or a subscription on Free. The commerce fee is separate from the $0 plan: 3.5% plus $0.30 per USD sale. Creator, if you need sequences now, is $39 a month up to 1,000 subscribers. That $39 is not required to broadcast.

**beehiiv Free** is $0 up to 2,500 subscribers and unlimited sends, with a site and the recommendation network. Automations, paid subscriptions, the ad network, and custom domains start on Lite. Lite at the up-to-1,000 bucket is $59 a month, or $588 for the year. Stay on Free while 2,500 is enough and Free already does the job.

**Substack** is $0 to publish at any list size. Paid subscriptions cost 10% of subscription revenue, plus Stripe. A custom domain is $50 once. If you will not charge readers, there is no monthly platform bill to look up. The help article was updated 29 October 2026 and re-read 25 November 2026.

**MailerLite Free** is 250 active subscribers and 2,500 emails a month. That is a real free plan, and it is a small one. Comfort at 500 subscribers is $12 a month. At 1,000 subscribers Comfort is $19 a month.

**Mailchimp Free** is 250 contacts and 500 sends a month, with a daily cap of 250, as read on 24 November 2026. Essentials starts at $13 a month and Standard at $20, both for up to 500 contacts. Above that, check current pricing.

**Flodesk's free plan does not send.** The pricing FAQ, re-read 25 November 2026, says it is for forms, landing pages, and a link-in-bio page, and that email sending is not included. The first sending plan is Email at $46 a month, or $456 for the year. A free Flodesk account is not a free newsletter.

**Ghost(Pro) has no hosted free plan.** Starter is $18 a month, or $180 for the year, up to 1,000 members, and the pricing page marks paid subscriptions as not included. Publisher, which can charge members and takes 0% of payments, is $35 a month at 1,000 members, or $348 for the year.

**MailCheap has no hosted free plan.** You can draft without paying. Sending, the public signup form, and your own domain need a plan. Starter is $19 a month for 20,000 emails and up to 10,000 subscribers, or $190 for the year. Yearly billing costs the price of 10 months. If Kit Free or beehiiv Free already sends the issue, Starter is an extra bill, not a step you owe the process.

Create a newsletter for free by picking the $0 row that matches the feature. How to make a newsletter on a free plan is the same issue you would write on a paid plan. The upgrade is for a missing feature.

At 5,000 people, the ordinary 4.33-week month is already about 21,700 emails, over Starter, so the row is Growth at $39.

<h2 id="issue">5. How to create a newsletter issue, and the welcome</h2>

How to create an email newsletter, as writing, is one job per issue. How to create a newsletter people finish is that job, not a design system. How to make a newsletter in the editor is the same job with the tool's blocks. A visual tutorial can wait.

How to write an email newsletter that matches the signup promise:

1. A from-name a stranger can recognize, on an address at a domain you control once you have one.
2. A subject line that describes the issue. A clever subject that hides the topic trains people to ignore the next one.
3. The point, in the first screen, before a logo and a stack of links.
4. Links you have clicked yourself.
5. A sign-off that says when the next issue comes.
6. A visible way to unsubscribe.
7. A postal address on commercial mail. The legal section says why.

One shop, one book, and one link can be the whole issue. How to send an email newsletter is the platform's send button, after you have mailed yourself and then ten people who will say if a link breaks.

The welcome email goes once to each new person, before you announce the letter. Restate the promise, the schedule, and how to leave. On Kit Free you can broadcast, and a sequence needs Creator, so the first month can be a welcome you send by hand if the volume is small. On MailCheap, welcome and confirmation emails count toward the cap. Turn the welcome on before the announcement. Do not build week one as a paywall unless charging is the product. The last section is the short version.

<h2 id="signup">6. Newsletter sign up: one form, one page</h2>

A newsletter sign up fails when the form is in five places and the sentence above it is different in each. Use one form, in one place you already have an audience. That place can be an existing site, a checkout receipt, or a landing page the email tool hosts. Kit, MailerLite, beehiiv, and Flodesk all host pages. Flodesk's free plan can host the page and still cannot send the letter. Do not collect addresses on a tool that cannot mail them, unless you already know which sender you will connect.

The page needs the promise, the schedule, and the form. Say what the next issue is. If you use double opt-in, say they must click the confirmation. People who miss that click are not subscribers yet.

Sign up from your own address before you share the page. Check the confirmation, the welcome, and the footer. Then send the link to those ten people. Launch the newsletter to people who already asked, on the day you named.

You need a URL for the form, not a blog, a shop, and a podcast. A newsletter website, the public archive, can come later. Several of these tools host it. If you already have a site, put the form there.

<h2 id="growth">7. Newsletter growth from people who asked</h2>

Newsletter subscribers are an asset only if they wanted the letter. Newsletter growth, in the first months, is one channel you already use: the shop counter, the last slide of a talk, the receipt, the site you have had for a year. Ask once, with the promise sentence, and a reason to join this week rather than "someday."

Do not buy a list, scrape one, or add buyers because consent was buried in a footer. Inbox providers treat that as spam whether or not the software blocks the account. MailCheap reviews who is sending before the first issues, and the plans page says a new account it cannot take is canceled and the first payment refunded. That review is not a substitute for permission.

Import only people who asked, and keep the file. A move carries addresses, not automations, paid billing, or a recommendation network. The [platform guide](/guides/newsletter-platforms/) notes what does not transfer. The [alternatives hub](/alternatives/) is for when you already pay a tool. One list and one schedule, until the calendar proves you can keep two.

<h2 id="domain">8. Set the sending domain before the list is large</h2>

Buy a domain you control if you expect to leave the first platform. A shared sending domain is fine for a test and a poor long-term from-address.

Substack charges $50 once for a custom domain. beehiiv includes custom domains on paid plans, not on Free. MailCheap includes your own domain on a paid plan. Ghost's pricing page, read 25 November 2026, lists a custom website domain on Starter, and lists the custom sending domain (the domain on the email) on Publisher, not on Starter. Those are different settings. A website can live at your domain while the email still leaves from the host. For deliverability you care about the sending domain.

Three DNS records are the usual set. You add them at the company that holds the domain. The email platform shows the values. This guide does not paste sample records, because a wrong record is worse than none.

**SPF** lists servers allowed to send for the domain. If the newsletter tool is missing, receivers are more likely to treat the message as forged.

**DKIM** is a signature receivers use to check that the message still matches what the domain owner signed. Google's sender guidelines ask for a key of at least 1024 bits for mail to personal Gmail accounts, and recommend 2048 bits where the registrar supports it.

**DMARC** says what to do when SPF or DKIM fails, and it requires the authenticated domain to line up with the From address. A policy of `p=none` monitors. Quarantine and reject are stricter. Read the reports before you jump to reject.

Google's [email sender guidelines](https://support.google.com/a/answer/81126) split all senders from bulk senders. All senders need SPF or DKIM, and a spam rate under 0.3% in Postmaster Tools. More than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail accounts also needs SPF and DKIM, DMARC of at least `p=none`, alignment with the From domain, and one-click unsubscribe on marketing mail. Yahoo's [sender best practices](https://senders.yahooinc.com/best-practices/) ask all senders for SPF or DKIM and a spam rate under 0.3%. Bulk senders there need both SPF and DKIM, DMARC of at least `p=none`, one-click unsubscribe, a visible unsubscribe, and opt-outs honored within 2 days. A 200-person letter is not that bulk tier. Set the records anyway. Mail yourself from the new domain before you announce it.

<h2 id="legal">9. Commercial email rules: CAN-SPAM, unsubscribe, double opt-in</h2>

This is a reading of public guidance, not a legal opinion. Rules outside the United States, including in the European Union, can be stricter. The US baseline is the FTC's [CAN-SPAM compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business).

The guide's requirements, in short, are these. Do not use false or misleading header information. Do not use a deceptive subject line. Identify the message as an ad if it is one. Include a valid physical postal address. Tell people how to opt out of future commercial email. Honor opt-out requests within 10 business days. Monitor what other people do on your behalf, including an agency or a freelancer who sends under your name. A receipt or a similar transactional message is treated differently from a promotional issue. If the issue is the product pitch, assume the commercial rules apply.

Unsubscribe has to work. The footer link is the visible version. One-click unsubscribe is the mailbox button Google and Yahoo require of bulk senders. Confirm it on a test to Gmail. CAN-SPAM allows 10 business days. Yahoo's bulk rule says 2 days. Meet the shorter one if you might be in that bulk group. Do not make people log in to leave.

Double opt-in is the extra click: they submit the form, then confirm from the inbox. CAN-SPAM does not require it. Yahoo recommends that click so typos and bots stay off the list. Kit, beehiiv, MailerLite, and MailCheap can send the confirmation. MailCheap counts it toward the email allowance. Use it on a public form. A purchased list fails this section. Do not import one.

<h2 id="metrics">10. What to measure after the first sends</h2>

Measure the numbers that tell you whether to keep the schedule, not a score you can post.

**Delivered, bounced, and complained.** Bounces mean the address was bad. Complaints mean someone marked the mail as spam. Google and Yahoo both talk about a spam rate under 0.3%. You do not need a dashboard metaphor to act on a spike. Stop, find the source of the addresses, and do not send the next issue to that segment until you know.

**Unsubscribes.** A few after a sharp issue are normal. A jump after you changed the schedule or the topic means the promise on the signup page and the issue diverged. Fix the promise or fix the issue. Do not email the people who left.

**Clicks.** They show that a link was the point of the issue and that it worked. They are a cleaner signal than opens.

**Opens.** Some inboxes fetch images before a person reads, so the rate can move without a reader. Treat it as a rough trend. This page does not quote an average open rate. We did not measure one.

**List growth.** New confirmed subscribers, minus unsubscribes and bounces, over a month. If that is flat and the issue matches the promise, the form is in the wrong place or the sentence is vague. That is not a reason to buy addresses. Kit, beehiiv, Ghost, MailerLite, Flodesk, Substack, Mailchimp, and MailCheap all show some version of these counts. The labels differ.

<h2 id="money">11. A short note on charging readers</h2>

You can start free and charge later only if the tool can sell the subscription when you are ready. Substack can, and then keeps 10% plus Stripe. Kit can, including on Free, at 3.5% plus $0.30 per USD sale. beehiiv can on Lite and Pro, not on Free, and takes 0% of the payment on those paid plans. Ghost can on Publisher and Business, not on Starter, at 0%. MailerLite can on Comfort and Power, not on Free. Flodesk can on the Everything plan, not on Email or Free. MailCheap cannot sell subscriptions at all. You can still mention a sponsor or link to a checkout you run somewhere else.

If you already know you will charge inside the tool this year, start on a tool that can. A free-list move is a CSV. A paid relationship is a new checkout unless the Stripe account is yours. The fees at $1,000, $5,000, and $10,000 a month are on [newsletter monetization](/guides/newsletter-monetization/). The software bill is on [newsletter cost](/guides/newsletter-cost/). Come back when the list crosses 2,500 subscribers on beehiiv, 10,000 on Kit, or the MailCheap email cap. A $0 plan that still sends is still the right plan.
