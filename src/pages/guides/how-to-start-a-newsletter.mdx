  <h2>Write the promise in one sentence. Then pick the cheapest platform that can send it.</h2>
  <p>If the promise is “one letter a week to people who asked,” start on <strong>Kit or beehiiv’s free plan, or on Substack</strong>. If the promise is “a paid membership with a site,” start on <strong>Ghost or beehiiv Lite</strong> once you are ready to charge, not before. If the promise is “I already have a list and I want the lowest bill for a regular send,” then look at <strong>MailCheap</strong>, and not before you have the list. The rest of this guide is the work that makes that choice obvious.</p>

## The reader and the promise

A newsletter is a repeating email a specific person expects. That definition is dull and it removes half the software market. Before you create an account, write three lines on a note that is not inside a platform:

1. Who it is for, in terms you could use to decline a subscriber. “People who run independent newsletters” is a start. “Everyone who likes ideas” is how you end up with a list you are afraid to mail.
2. What they get, and what they do not. A Sunday briefing of three links is a product. A Sunday briefing plus a community plus a podcast plus a course is four products, and you will under-build all of them.
3. Why it arrives by email instead of as a post on a site they might not revisit. Email is a push. If you do not want to show up in an inbox on a schedule, you want a blog. Ghost can be that blog. A newsletter tool will keep asking you for the next issue.

Name the letter. Pick a from-name that will still make sense in two years. Avoid a pun you have to explain in the footer. You will type it in a lot of signup forms.

If you cannot write the three lines, do not pick a platform to force a decision. The platform’s onboarding will offer you a growth network, a theme, and a stripe connect button. Those are answers to questions you have not asked. The writers who are still publishing a year later are usually the ones who sent the same kind of issue ten times, not the ones who chose the most sophisticated stack in week one.

## Cadence before software

Cadence is a pricing input. A weekly issue to 2,000 people is about 8,667 emails a month, using subscribers × issues per week × 52 ÷ 12. A daily issue to the same list is about 60,667 emails. Tools that charge per subscriber do not care. Tools that charge per email, or that cap emails at a multiple of the tier, care a great deal. MailCheap Starter’s 20,000 emails cover the weekly and not the daily. MailerLite Comfort at the 2,500 tier allows 25,000 emails and also fails the daily. beehiiv, Kit’s paid plans, Ghost, and Substack do not meter the send.

Promise a cadence you can keep during a bad week. Weekly is the default we would set for almost every new letter. Biweekly is honest if your reporting takes longer. Daily is a job. “Whenever I have something to say” trains readers to ignore you, and it makes the cost calculator useless because you do not know the inputs. Pick a weekday. Put it on the calendar as an appointment with a draft, not as a hope. The first issue can be short. It cannot be missing.

The [calculator](/#calculator) on this site is there for the moment the cadence and the imagined list size are both real. Use it after you have the sentence, not before. A slider is not a strategy.

## Choose a platform for the job you have

Match the job, then the price. Prices below were checked on 24 November 2026.

**You are starting from zero and you will not charge readers yet.** Use a free hosted plan. Kit Free covers up to 10,000 subscribers with broadcasts, forms, and landing pages, and withholds visual automations. beehiiv Free covers up to 2,500 with unlimited sends, a simple site, and recommendations, and withholds automations, ads, and paid subscriptions. Substack covers any size at $0 until you flip on paid, then keeps 10%. MailerLite Free only reaches 250 people and 2,500 emails. Mailchimp Free is 250 contacts and 500 sends. MailCheap has no free hosted plan. Starting there at $19 is paying to avoid a free tool that fits. Do not do it because a comparison site has an affiliate link. This one does, and the advice is still to start free.

**You know you will charge for the writing.** Substack is the lowest friction and the highest percentage. beehiiv Lite adds a monthly bill and a 0% platform fee, and it is wasted money until the list or the feature set needs it. Ghost(Pro) Publisher is the membership site, from $35 a month at 1,000 members. MailerLite Comfort can sell a paid newsletter from $12 a month at 500 subscribers. Kit can sell subscriptions, including on the free plan. Pick one. Connecting Stripe on four of them is how you confuse the three people who were willing to pay.

**You already have thousands of subscribers and a weekly send.** Now price the paid field. At 4,000 weekly subscribers, Kit is still $0 for broadcasts, MailCheap Starter is $19, MailerLite Comfort is $49 at the 5,000 tier, Ghost Publisher is $75, and beehiiv Lite is $109. Substack is $0 until revenue. The “best” row is the one whose missing features you will not notice. Our [Mailchimp](/alternatives/mailchimp/), [Kit](/alternatives/convertkit/), [beehiiv](/alternatives/beehiiv/), [Ghost](/alternatives/ghost/), and [Substack](/alternatives/substack/) guides are the long versions of each exit. The [beehiiv pricing](/pricing/beehiiv/) page is the tier table if that is the finalist.

**You want the platform to grow the list for you.** Then you are choosing a network: Substack’s, beehiiv’s, or Kit’s. MailCheap does not have one. A cheap send does not substitute for a recommendation engine. Pay for the network or do the distribution yourself, on purpose, with your own links. Hoping a $19 tool will behave like beehiiv is how people write angry migrations.

**You want a publication website.** Ghost. Not a newsletter tool with a landing page, unless you have looked at beehiiv’s site builder and it is enough. Be honest about whether anyone will visit the archive. Most new letters do not have a traffic problem. They have a “ten people asked to be emailed” problem. Solve that first.

## What the first year costs

Assume you end the year at 2,000 subscribers, one issue a week, and you do not charge anyone. Kit: $0. beehiiv: $0, because you are under 2,500. Substack: $0. MailerLite Comfort, if you refused the free cap and wanted the suite: $33 a month at the 2,500 tier, $396 a year. MailCheap, if you insisted on it: $19 a month, $228 a year, for a volume of about 8,667 emails that did not require a paid plan anywhere else. Ghost Starter: $18 a month for the site, and you still cannot sell memberships on Starter.

Now assume the letter works and you finish the year at 8,000 subscribers, still weekly, still free. Kit: $0. Substack: $0. beehiiv: the 10,000 bucket, Lite at $139 a month, because Free ended at 2,500. That is $1,668 if you pay monthly for the whole stretch you are over the cap, which you will not be — you cross 2,500 sometime mid-year. Budget the tier you will actually sit in, not twelve months of the final tier and not twelve months of Free. MailCheap Growth is $39, because 8,000 weekly is about 34,667 emails, over Starter’s 20,000. Ghost Publisher at the 10,000 band is $105. Comfort is $89.

The spread between $0 and $139 a month is not a rounding error. It is a feature decision. If you spent the year on beehiiv because you wanted recommendations, price the subscribers those recommendations added. If you cannot, you paid for a logo. The [how we test](/how-we-test/) page explains why we refuse to invent a growth percentage to paper over that.

Add costs that are not the platform. A domain is usually $10 to $20 a year at a registrar. A professional email address is optional. Stripe is free until someone pays. Your time is the largest cost and the one no pricing page admits. An hour a week for a year is fifty hours. Choose a tool that does not ask for ten of those hours in settings.

## The setup that matters

Do these in order. They are more important than the theme.

1. Buy a domain you control, even if the platform gives you a subdomain. Point a `letter.` or the root at the tool when you are ready. Keep the DNS login somewhere that is not your memory.
2. Create the account on the free plan you chose. Skip the paid trial unless a specific paid feature is required for issue one. Trials convert into forgotten subscriptions.
3. Add SPF, DKIM, and a DMARC record the platform documents. Send a test to a Gmail inbox and another provider. Open the message and look at the authentication results. This is the difference between a newsletter and a spam folder.
4. Write a signup page that repeats the promise. Say how often you send. Say what you will not send. A vague “join the community” collects addresses you should not have.
5. Use double opt-in if the platform offers it and your audience will tolerate the extra click. It cuts fake addresses. It also cuts lazy ones. For a brand-new letter, clean beats large.
6. Write a welcome email that delivers something useful the same day. One tip, one link, one sentence about what happens next. Do not attach a five-email course you have not written.
7. Add a physical mailing address if the law that applies to you requires it. US commercial email has had that expectation for a long time. Platforms often put a footer field in your face for this reason. Fill it in with a real address you are willing to publish, even if that is a mailbox service.
8. Put the affiliate and sponsor policy in a drawer. You do not need a media kit for issue one. You need an issue.

If you later move to MailCheap, imports need a paid plan and a CSV of people who consented. That is a later problem. Do not start on a paid import flow with an empty spreadsheet.

## The first ten issues

Issue one will feel loud in your head and quiet in the world. Send it anyway. Ten issues is the smallest sample that tells you whether the promise was right. Keep the structure stable so readers learn it. Change the structure when you are bored of it and they are not, which is the dangerous direction, or when they cannot use it, which is the useful direction. Reply to replies. The inbox is the research.

Do not buy a growth tool in week two because the list is forty people. Forty people who asked is a better asset than four hundred who do not remember you. Recommendations and referral programs are reasonable after the letter is unmistakably itself. They are a way to multiply a fog if you turn them on first.

Track three numbers and ignore the rest until issue ten: subscribers who confirmed, the share of the issue that gets opened if your tool’s open rate is even roughly honest, and replies. Open rates are a compromised metric. Replies are not. A letter that gets replies can be improved. A letter that only gets a dashboard cannot.

When the list crosses a free cap, return to this page and to the calculator. The upgrade is a new decision. It is not a failure. beehiiv Free ending at 2,500 is a planned moment. Kit’s cliff at 10,000 is a planned moment. Substack’s 10% begins the day you want a paywall, which should be a planned moment too. Write down what the paywall includes that the free letter does not. “Support my work” is a donation. It can work. It is clearer when the paid edition is a thing.

## Mistakes that cost money

Paying for MailCheap, Creator, or Lite before a free plan has failed you. Importing a list you bought, or a list of people who subscribed to something else. Those lists hurt deliverability and, on the tools whose terms we read, they can close the account. Building five automations before one issue exists. Choosing a tool because a YouTube sponsorship said it was taking over. Comparing annual prices to monthly prices and calling the difference a discount you earned. Ignoring a send cap because the homepage said “unlimited” on a different plan. Starting a daily. Quitting at issue three because a platform with a network did not make you famous.

None of these are moral failures. They are how the pricing pages are written. The pages want you in a tier. You want a letter that shows up. Start with the letter. The [home page](/) will still be here when the list is large enough to have a bill worth arguing about.

If you already have the list and the bill, you are not starting. You are switching. Use the roundups. They are more useful than this page once the CSV exists, and they are more specific about who should not use the cheap option. The short version, repeated because it is the one people skip: MailCheap is for a regular sender who does not need paid subscriptions, ads, referrals, or tests, and who cannot use a free plan. Everyone else should spend the $19 on the domain and the next ten issues.
